# w6: lecture notes + exam questions

This week's class was spend in reviewing prior material.
Most of the night re-ran
gloss4 (cuts, `least`, one-pass cuts, `Tree`).

**N1. Trees give plans, not just predictions.** Drop your design
into the tree. Find the best leaf (marked ▲). Walk up from your
leaf to the shared ancestor, then down to ▲: the tests you must
flip are the plan. In the auto93 demo, a car with volume in
(156, 262] had to get under 112, then under 90, to reach the
best leaf. Then take it to the business user and argue: is the
gain worth it, and is a car that small still a car? (At some
point it is a motorcycle.) That is a short, evidence-based
conversation. An LLM asked "why?" gives 600 lines of text.

**N2. Fastmap vs PCA, and the shoebox.** PCA is the parametric
way to project data: eigenvectors of the correlation matrix,
numbers only. Fastmap needs only a distance function, so it
works on mixed numbers and symbols. Instance-based learning
("put every row in a shoebox; answer a new row from its nearest
neighbors") costs a scan of the whole box per query. Cluster
once instead: a recursive fastmap tree is also an index, so a
new row reaches its neighborhood in about log2(n) steps
(approximately: the true nearest neighbor can sit just across
a split).

**N3. Reading timm's code.** `i` is always self (the object a
method works on). `t` is a table, `fun` a function, and type
hints sit in comments. The `eg` table maps command-line flags
to functions: `eg["--mydemo"] = function() ... end` adds your
own experiment, and `--key=val` changes any setting (e.g.
`--leaf=100`). This is code you dive into, not code you skim.
Tactic for any unfamiliar language: ask an LLM to port it to a
language you know, with type hints, then read both.

**N4. Live Python: lambdas and a memo decorator.**
`sorted(a, key=lambda x: -x)` is the open-closed principle: the
sort is closed, the order is open. `def` is just a lambda body
written to a name. Naive `fib(40)` takes many seconds (the
calls grow exponentially); a memoizing decorator returns a
wrapper that closes over its own `cache` dict, and `fib(100)`
returns at once. Each call to the decorator makes a fresh cache,
so two decorated functions never share one. (The standard
library's `functools.cache` does the same.)

**N5. "Is this relevant to industry?"** A student asked: in
industry you cannot keep up by coding by hand. timm's answer:
true today, but code review is the job that grows, and AI slop
is real (some open-source projects now restrict AI-written
contributions). Aim to be the senior engineer whose technical
opinion changes what the room does, not the replaceable one who
picks up a stack in two months. Go very deep on at least one
thing. Two more claims: *neuro-symbolic* (the neural layer is a
great interface and tutor, but under it you still want exact
symbolic tools: matrix inversion, 1+1), and *micro-AI* (small
models that only classify or regress are much faster and
cheaper than general chat models, "prod, not God").

**N6. The bitter lesson, and its bill.** Sutton (2019): across
chess, Go, speech and vision, general methods plus more
compute beat hand-built cleverness. timm's objections: every
one of those examples gets instant feedback (the game is won,
the word is right), and much of SE does not. The bill: data
centers, water, and restarted nuclear plants (Microsoft's deal
to restart Three Mile Island Unit 1). And a skills bill:
Bainbridge's *Ironies of Automation* (1983) warns that
automating the easy parts leaves humans the hard parts, without
the practice that built their skill. Where do senior engineers
come from?

## Exam questions

**Q1. Heaven, by hand**

a) Goals: minimize cost, maximize speed, minimize weight. Write
the heaven vector, and say why each goal is normalized before
any distance is taken.

b) The header below names three normalized goals. Which row is
closer to heaven, and which goal contributes most to row A's
distance?

```
        Lbs-  Acc+  Mpg+
row A   0.9   0.5   0.1
row B   0.2   0.6   0.7
```

**Q2. Four reasons to stop**

a) List the four conditions under which a tree node becomes a
leaf.

b) The guard `#yes > 0 and #no > 0` is deleted, and one cut
sends every row to `yes`. Using `NUM.mid` (an empty Num has
`mu = 0`), explain how the empty `no` child could end up marked
as the best leaf in the tree.

**Q3. The leaf dial**

a) What does `the.leaf` control, and what did the demo show as
it went from 100 to 25?

b) You will use the tree to classify NEW rows. Argue for a leaf
of about 20 over a leaf of 3, in terms of how much evidence each
leaf speaks for.

**Q4. Greedy, and how it fails**

a) Define a greedy search, and say what ezr's tree never does
after it picks a cut.

b) Rows are good exactly when `a` and `b` differ, as below.
Explain why the first cut on either column alone looks useless,
and what a learner with one step of look-ahead would find.

```
 a  b  good?
 0  0   no
 0  1   yes
 1  0   yes
 1  1   no
```

**Q5. A plan from a tree**

a) Describe, in two steps, how to read a plan out of a tree.

b) Your car has `Volume` 200. Using the tree below, write the
plan to reach ▲, and state one question you must put to the
business user before anyone acts on it.

```
Volume <= 112
|  Volume <= 90  .......... Mpg+ 33  ▲
|  Volume >  90  .......... Mpg+ 28
Volume >  112
|  Volume <= 262 .......... Mpg+ 21
|  Volume >  262 .......... Mpg+ 15  ▼
```

**Q6. The shoebox**

a) PCA projects data using the eigenvectors of a correlation
matrix. Name the one thing fastmap needs instead, and say which
of the two can run on a table holding both numbers and symbols.

b) A shoebox holds 10,000 rows and 1,000 queries arrive. Compare
the distance calculations for brute-force nearest neighbor
against descending a fastmap tree, and say what the tree gives
up in exchange.

**Q7. The bitter lesson**

a) State Sutton's bitter lesson and Bainbridge's irony of
automation, one sentence each.

b) timm says every example in the bitter lesson has instant
feedback. Name one SE task without instant feedback, and explain
why "just add compute" struggles there.

## Answers

*A practice bank. These are not the exam.*

**A1.** (0, 1, 0): lowest cost, highest speed, lowest weight.
Normalize first because raw units differ (thousands of pounds
against tens of mpg); without it, the goal with the biggest
numbers decides every distance. For (b), the header marks give
heaven = (0, 1, 1): `Lbs-` minimized, `Acc+` and `Mpg+`
maximized. A's gaps are 0.9, 0.5, 0.9; B's are 0.2, 0.4, 0.3.
B is much closer. A's `Lbs-` and `Mpg+` gaps tie for most
(0.81 each, squared).

**A2.** Fewer than `2*the.leaf` rows; depth reached `the.maxd`;
`bestcut` found no cut; one side of the division is empty. With
the guard gone, `recurse` builds a node from zero rows. Its `ys`
is an empty Num, so `mu = 0`, and with disty a distance of 0 is
heaven itself. `t.val = min(t.yes.val, t.no.val)` then carries
that fake perfect score upward, and the printer marks the empty
leaf as best.

**A3.** The minimum rows allowed on each side of a cut. As it
fell from 100 to 50 to 25, the tree grew from a stump to a
deeper tree with sharper best and worst leaves. A leaf of 3
predicts from three rows, so one odd row moves the answer: it
memorizes. A leaf of 20 averages more evidence, so new rows get
an answer backed by support, not by accident.

**A4.** A greedy search takes the best-looking step now and
never goes back to change it; ezr never revisits a cut. In the
table, splitting on `a` alone leaves each side half good, half
bad, and the same for `b`: no cut improves diversity. Look-ahead
tries `a` then `b` together and finds two pure pairs: the
target is `a != b`, which needs both columns.

**A5.** Find your leaf and the best leaf; walk up to their
shared ancestor and down to the best: the tests you cross are
the changes. `Volume` 200 sits at `Mpg+` 21. Plan: get `Volume`
under 112 (cross the root), then under 90. Question: is a car
under 90 still something customers will buy (is it still a
car), and is the cost of that redesign worth 33 against 21 mpg?

**A6.** Fastmap needs only a distance between two rows. That is
why fastmap runs on mixed numbers and symbols, while PCA needs
numeric columns to build its correlation matrix. Brute force:
10,000 distances per query, 10 million in all. Tree: about
2·log2(10,000) ≈ 28 distances per query (two poles per level).
The price: the descent is approximate; a true nearest neighbor
just across a split is missed.

**A7.** Bitter lesson: general methods that scale with compute
beat methods built on human knowledge, in the long run. Irony
of automation: automating the easy work leaves people the hard
work, without the practice that made them able to do it. Example
task: software effort estimation, or architecture choice. Truth
arrives months or years later, each label (a finished project)
is costly, and there are few of them, so a compute-hungry search
has little feedback to learn from. That is the gap active
learning and small arguable models aim at.
